# The Water Cycle: Research Notes

## Key Facts

- The sun drives the water cycle by supplying the heat that makes water evaporate. Source: USGS Water Science School, "The Sun and the Water Cycle," https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/sun-and-water-cycle
- About 71% of Earth's surface is covered by water, and the oceans hold about 96.5% of all Earth's water. Source: USGS Water Science School, "How Much Water is There on Earth?", https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/how-much-water-there-earth
- The oceans supply about 90% of the evaporated water that enters the water cycle. Source: USGS Water Science School, "Oceans and Seas and the Water Cycle," https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/oceans-and-seas-and-water-cycle
- Plants add about 10% of the moisture in the atmosphere through transpiration. Source: USGS, "Evapotranspiration and the Water Cycle" (copy hosted by Columbia University IRI), https://fdacourse.iri.columbia.edu/documents/M1U2_Clouds/Clouds_Readings/S3_Clouds_Readings/S3_Evapotranspiration_The_Water_Cycle_USGS.pdf
- Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh, and almost all of it is locked in ice or stored underground as groundwater. Source: USGS Water Science School, "Where is Earth's Water?", https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/where-earths-water
- Water vapor stays in the atmosphere for about 8 to 10 days on average before falling as precipitation. Source: Gimeno et al. (2021), "The residence time of water vapour in the atmosphere," Nature Reviews Earth & Environment, via University of Arizona, https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-residence-time-of-water-vapour-in-the-atmosphere/
- NOAA notes that the simple loop of evaporation, condensation, and precipitation is a useful model, but the real cycle is more complicated. Source: NOAA, "Water cycle," https://noaa.gov/node/6423

## Main Stages of the Water Cycle (in order)

1. Evaporation: the sun heats surface water, which turns into water vapor and rises. Source: NOAA National Weather Service, "The Hydrologic Cycle," https://governmentshutdown.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-01/hydrocycle_2x3.pdf
2. Transpiration: plants release water vapor from their leaves into the air. Source: USGS, "Evapotranspiration and the Water Cycle," https://fdacourse.iri.columbia.edu/documents/M1U2_Clouds/Clouds_Readings/S3_Clouds_Readings/S3_Evapotranspiration_The_Water_Cycle_USGS.pdf
3. Condensation: water vapor cools and turns back into tiny droplets that form clouds. Source: NOAA National Weather Service, "The Hydrologic Cycle," https://governmentshutdown.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-01/hydrocycle_2x3.pdf
4. Precipitation: water falls back to Earth as rain, snow, sleet, drizzle, or hail. Source: NOAA National Weather Service, "The Hydrologic Cycle," https://governmentshutdown.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-01/hydrocycle_2x3.pdf
5. Runoff and infiltration: water flows over land into streams, lakes, and oceans, or soaks into the ground to become groundwater. Source: NOAA National Weather Service, "The Hydrologic Cycle," https://governmentshutdown.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-01/hydrocycle_2x3.pdf
6. Collection (storage): water gathers in oceans, lakes, ice, and groundwater until it evaporates again. Source: USGS Water Science School, "Where is Earth's Water?", https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/where-earths-water
