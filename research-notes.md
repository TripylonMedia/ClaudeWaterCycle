# The Water Cycle: Research Notes

## Key Facts

- Earth's water keeps moving and changing between liquid, solid (ice), and gas (water vapor). Source: USGS Water Science School, https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html
- The sun powers the water cycle by heating water so it evaporates. Source: USGS Water Science School, https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-beg.html
- Most evaporation comes from the oceans, which cover about 70% of Earth's surface. Source: USGS Water Science School, https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html
- The oceans hold about 96.5% of all Earth's water. Source: USGS, "The distribution of water on, in, and above the Earth," https://usgs.gov/media/images/distribution-water-and-above-earth
- Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is freshwater, and most of that is frozen in ice or stored underground. Source: USGS, "Where is Earth's Water?", https://usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/where-earths-water
- Oceans, seas, lakes, and rivers supply nearly 90% of the moisture in the air, and plant transpiration supplies about 10%. Source: Hubbard Brook Research Foundation, Water Cycle Glossary, https://hubbardbrook.org/waterviz/water-cycle-glossary/
- A water molecule stays in the atmosphere for about 9 days on average before falling as precipitation. Source: Science Learning Hub (NZ), "Age of water," https://www.sciencelearn.org.nz/videos/357-age-of-water

## Main Stages of the Water Cycle (in order)

1. Evaporation: the sun heats surface water, which turns into water vapor and rises. Source: NOAA National Weather Service JetStream, https://cdn.serc.carleton.edu/files/getsi/teaching_materials/water_resources/noaa_water_cycle_figure.pdf
2. Transpiration: plants release water vapor from their leaves into the air. Source: NOAA National Weather Service JetStream, https://cdn.serc.carleton.edu/files/getsi/teaching_materials/water_resources/noaa_water_cycle_figure.pdf
3. Condensation: the vapor cools higher in the atmosphere and forms tiny droplets that make clouds. Source: USGS Water Science School, https://water.usgs.gov/edu/watercycle-kids-int.html
4. Precipitation: water falls back to Earth as rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Source: NOAA National Weather Service JetStream, https://cdn.serc.carleton.edu/files/getsi/teaching_materials/water_resources/noaa_water_cycle_figure.pdf
5. Runoff and infiltration: water flows over land into streams, lakes, and oceans, or soaks into the ground to become groundwater. Source: NOAA National Weather Service JetStream, https://cdn.serc.carleton.edu/files/getsi/teaching_materials/water_resources/noaa_water_cycle_figure.pdf
6. Collection (storage): water gathers in oceans, lakes, ice, and groundwater, ready to evaporate again. Source: USGS, "Where is Earth's Water?", https://usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/where-earths-water

Note: NOAA says the simple circle of evaporation, condensation, and precipitation is a useful model, but the real cycle is more complicated. Source: NOAA, https://noaa.gov/node/6423
